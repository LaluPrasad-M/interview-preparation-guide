# How To Interview

> [!tldr]
> The non technical half of an interview: what to research before, the four kinds of question you will get, and the thank you note most people skip.

Everything here is a skill you can practise, not a personality you either have or do not have. Confidence, enthusiasm and thinking on your feet are the things interviewers say they are watching for, and all three get easier with rehearsal.

---

## Before the interview

**Research the company.** The website is the shallow end. Go to recent press releases, industry reports, their social media, and their customer reviews. You are trying to work out what they care about right now, so you can talk about this job rather than sounding like someone who wants any job.

**Understand the role.** Read the job description slowly. List the responsibilities and the skills it asks for, then use that list to guess the questions. It also tells you what "doing well" will mean if you get hired.

**Prepare your own questions.** The interview goes both ways, and you are also deciding whether you want to work there. Two that consistently land:

- What does success look like in this role in the first 90 days?
- What is the biggest challenge the team is facing now, and how would this role help with it?

## The four kinds of question

| Kind | What they are really asking | How to answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tell me about yourself | can you explain who you are without rambling | a 30 to 60 second pitch, rehearsed but not recited |
| Behavioural | what have you actually done | STAR, with numbers |
| Situational or technical | how do you think | name the concrete steps and why each one |
| General or personality | will we get along with you | honest, positive, short |

### Tell me about yourself

This usually comes first, and the usual mistake is answering it with your whole life story. The interviewer stops listening halfway through.

What they want is a short summary of who you are, what you do, and why you fit this role. Lead with the strengths that matter for this job, not the ones you are proudest of.

> [!tip] Write it down once
> A **Career Identity Statement** is one sentence pulling together your skills, your interests and what you actually enjoy. Write it once, then the pitch is just saying it out loud with a couple of examples attached.

### Behavioural, and the STAR method

**STAR** is four beats in order:

| Beat | What goes in it |
| --- | --- |
| **Situation** | the context, briefly |
| **Task** | the problem you owned |
| **Action** | what you did |
| **Results** | what happened, with a number if you have one |

The Results beat is where most answers go soft, and it is the one that decides whether they believe you.

> [!example]- The same answer, vague and specific
> Vague: "I helped save the company money."
>
> Specific: "I negotiated a 15 percent reduction in costs, which saved about 50,000 dollars a year, and quality did not drop."
>
> The second one is the same story. It just proves it happened, and it shows how you made the decision.

If you did not get a good outcome, say what you learned. That still counts as a Result.

### Situational and technical

These are hypotheticals, usually close to the job. They are not testing whether you know the answer, they are testing how you get to one.

So say the steps out loud: how you would measure success, what milestones you would set, who would own what. Explain why each step, not just what.

You can connect it to something you have actually done, but say so explicitly, and say what that experience tells you about this new problem.

### General and personality

The usual three: your strengths and weaknesses, where you see yourself in five years, and why you are leaving your current job.

Be honest, stay positive about people you have worked with, and keep it short. Speak slowly, especially if you are nervous, because nervous people speed up and stop being understood.

---

## Practise out loud

Knowing the four categories is not the same as being able to answer them. The answers have to come out of your mouth fluently, and that only happens after you have said them a few times.

Practise enough to be fluent, and then stop. Past a certain point you start sounding like you are reciting, which is worse than being a bit rough.

Three ways to do it: with a friend, a mentor or a career counsellor; recording yourself and watching it back; or with an AI tool that will hold a conversation and give you feedback on the spot.

---

## Afterwards, within 24 hours

Send a short thank you note or email, and mention something specific you enjoyed talking about.

The 24 hour window matters because the conversation is still fresh for both of you. It is also your chance to add the thing you wish you had said.

> [!tip] No email address for them
> Ask the recruiter or your HR contact to pass it on. That works fine and nobody minds.
